Resume

Build a resume that actually gets read

Keep it to one page as an undergrad
Recruiters scan resumes in seconds. One page forces you to keep only what's relevant: your degree, 2–4 relevant experiences, and skills that match the role. A two-page resume for an internship application usually works against you.
Lead every bullet with what you did and what changed

Use the pattern: action + what you did + the result, with a number where you can.

  • Weak: "Helped with marketing for the club"
  • Strong: "Ran social accounts for a 200-member student org, growing followers 40% in one semester"
No work experience yet? Use what you actually have
Class projects, student organization roles, part-time jobs, and volunteer work all count if you describe the skill, not just the title. "Treasurer, Student Government" is a title; "Managed a $12,000 annual budget across 8 departments" is a skill.

Interviewing

Interview prep that's worth your time

Have 3 stories ready before any interview
Most behavioral questions ("tell me about a time you...") map to a handful of themes: handling conflict, leading something, fixing a mistake, and working under pressure. Prepare one real story for each, structured as situation → action → result, and you'll be ready for most of what comes up.
Research the company past the "About" page
Know their product, a recent news item about them, and why their specific role interests you. "I want to work in marketing" is generic; "I used your app and noticed X, and I'd want to work on that" shows you actually looked.
Always have questions ready for them
Ask about the team you'd join, what success looks like in the first 90 days, or what's changed recently. Skipping this, or asking only about pay and benefits this early, is one of the most common interview mistakes.
Choosing a direction

If you don't know what you want yet

Talk to people doing the job, not just the title

A 20-minute conversation with someone actually doing a job tells you more than a job description ever will. Most people say yes to a short, specific ask.

Try before you commit a career to it

A short internship, a part-time role, or even a single class project in a field can tell you whether you'd actually want to do it for years.

Your major doesn't lock you in

Most entry-level roles outside a handful of licensed fields (engineering, nursing, accounting) care more about what you can show than your exact major.
