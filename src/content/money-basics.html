Student finances

The practical, everyday money questions financial aid tools don't cover — what to do with a refund check, how to build a simple budget, what taxes actually apply to you, and how to start banking and earning smartly as a student.

This page is general education, not personalized financial or tax advice. For anything specific to your own situation, use the official sources linked below or talk to your school's financial aid office or a licensed tax professional.
Your financial aid refund

What a refund check actually is

When your financial aid (grants, scholarships, loans) adds up to more than what your school charges for tuition, fees and on-campus housing, the school pays itself first and sends you the leftover amount — usually by direct deposit. That leftover amount is your refund.

It's not free money

A refund is often made up partly of borrowed loan money, not just grants. Spending it like a windfall can mean paying interest later on money you've already spent on things that weren't essential.

The most common mistake

Treating the first deposit as a lump sum to spend in the first few weeks — on travel, electronics, or going out — and then being short on rent or groceries later in the semester.

A simple way to handle it

Before spending a financial aid refund, map out authorized education and living costs for the semester, such as course materials, food, transportation and housing. A federal student loan refund remains loan money restricted to authorized educational expenses; it is not unrestricted discretionary income. Ask your school's financial aid office about returning money you do not need.

If a refund includes loan money

The loan portion must be repaid under that loan's terms. Interest on Direct Unsubsidized Loans generally accrues while you are in school, even when payments are deferred. Borrow only what you need and contact your financial aid office or servicer about how and when to return unused funds.

Budgeting basics

A simple framework, plus a reset tool

You don't need a complicated spreadsheet to budget well as a student — you need a simple way to separate needs from wants, and a reset you can reach for when spending gets off track.

Needs first
Rent, groceries, utilities, transportation and required course materials get paid before anything else.
Savings next
Even a small, consistent amount set aside each time money comes in adds up, and builds the habit early.
Wants last
Whatever's left after needs and savings is genuinely free to spend on everything else — guilt-free.

Try a percentage-based split

A common starting framework is to split income roughly into needs, wants and savings/debt paydown, in that order of priority, and adjust the exact percentages to fit your own costs rather than following any split rigidly.

Track where money actually goes

Most banking apps already categorize your spending automatically. Spending ten minutes a week actually looking at it is often more useful than building a perfect budget you never check again.

Try a no-spend week or month

If spending feels out of control, pick a short, defined window — a week, or a full month — where you only pay for true essentials (rent, groceries, bills) and nothing else. It's a reset, not a punishment: the point is to notice what you don't actually miss.

Make the no-spend period realistic

Plan around things you can't avoid (a friend's birthday, a required textbook) rather than pretending they won't happen. A no-spend window you can actually finish teaches you more than an all-or-nothing one you abandon after two days.

Taxes for students

A general orientation, not tax advice

Taxes can feel intimidating the first time you file on your own. Here's the general shape of it — for anything specific to your situation, use the official IRS resources below or talk to a tax professional.

You may need to file a return

Many working students need to file a federal income tax return, depending on how much they earned and other factors like whether someone else claims them as a dependent. The rules depend on your specific income and situation, so check the official guidance rather than assuming either way.

Visit irs.gov →

Know your forms: W-2 and 1098-T

Employees generally receive a Form W-2. Gig or contract income may be reported on a 1099 form and can still be taxable even if you receive no form. Eligible schools generally issue Form 1098-T for reportable tuition transactions, but exceptions apply. Keep your forms and payment records, and ask your school if you expect a 1098-T but have not received it.

IRS Free File

The IRS partners with tax software providers to offer free guided tax prep for eligible taxpayers through its Free File program. It's worth checking before paying for commercial tax software.

Check Free File eligibility →

Education tax credits exist

Credits like the American Opportunity Tax Credit can reduce what you (or whoever claims you) owe based on education expenses. Whether you qualify depends on your specific situation, so look into it through a tax prep tool's guided questions or official IRS guidance rather than assuming eligibility.

Saving & banking

Setting up accounts that actually work for you

Checking vs. savings

A checking account is for everyday spending and bills; a savings account is meant to hold money you're not touching day-to-day and often earns a small amount of interest. Keeping them separate makes it harder to accidentally spend money you meant to save.

Automate a small transfer

Setting up an automatic transfer of even a small, fixed amount into savings each time you're paid removes the decision-making — you save before you have the chance to spend it.

Watch for monthly fees

Many banks waive monthly maintenance fees entirely for students, or with proof of a student ID, direct deposit, or a minimum balance. Ask directly, or look for an account explicitly marketed as a student account, before accepting a fee as unavoidable.

Build a small cushion

Even a modest emergency cushion — enough to cover a surprise expense like a car repair or a replaced phone — can keep a one-time problem from turning into debt. Building it gradually, a little at a time, is normal and realistic.

Part-time jobs & income strategy

Earning while you're still in school

Explore on-campus and work-study jobs

If your financial aid package includes Federal Work-Study, it's a part-time job you apply for like any other, usually on campus or with an approved employer, with wages paid directly to you as you work the hours. Check your award letter and your school's job board.

Review Federal Work-Study basics →

Look beyond campus too

Internships and part-time roles off campus can pay better or align more closely with your major than a generic campus job. Treat the search the same way you would a full job search.

Browse internships & jobs →

Balance hours with your course load

There's no universal "right" number of work hours — it depends on your course load, your major's workload, and how you handle the two at once. Start conservatively, especially in a harder semester, and adjust once you know how it feels.

It's resume-building, not just income

Even a part-time, on-campus job teaches real things employers care about — reliability, customer service, basic professional communication. It's worth describing on a resume the same way you would any other role.
