How FAFSA timing works

When to file, and why the exact date matters less than being early

The FAFSA opens once a year

The federal government opens the FAFSA application for the upcoming academic year on a set date each cycle, and it stays open for many months afterward. The exact opening date has shifted in recent cycles, so always confirm the current year's date on studentaid.gov rather than assuming it matches last year.

Check this year's timeline →

Your own school deadline comes first

The federal deadline is a last resort, not a target. Each college and each state sets its own, usually earlier, FAFSA deadline — miss that one and you can lose out on aid even if you're still inside the federal window.

Find your school’s financial aid resources →

Earlier is almost always better

File early using the tax year and information requested by that year's FAFSA. Provide consent for the IRS transfer when requested. If your household finances have changed or you cannot provide required information, contact your school's financial aid office for help rather than guessing.

File as soon as you can

You file again every year

FAFSA eligibility isn't permanent. You (and your family, if you're a dependent student) must submit a new FAFSA for every academic year you want aid, using the applicable tax year's information as instructed on the form.

Working while you study

What Federal Work-Study actually is

It's a job, not a grant

Federal Work-Study provides part-time job funding for students with financial need, generally on campus or with an approved off-campus employer such as a nonprofit or public agency. You still have to apply for and get the position like any other job — the program just subsidizes your wages.

Learn how it works →

Ask your school about work-study

Submit the FAFSA to be considered for Federal Work-Study. Your school determines eligibility and available funding. Check your financial aid offer, then ask the financial aid or student employment office how to apply for available jobs.

Read Federal Student Aid guidance →

Pay works differently from other aid

Work-study earnings are paid to you directly as you work the hours, generally at least minimum wage, rather than applied automatically to your tuition bill the way a grant or loan might be. Ask your financial aid office how your school handles it.

Find your school’s financial aid resources →

Not every school participates the same way

Not all jobs on campus are work-study positions, and not all students who want work-study are offered it — it depends on your school's funding and your demonstrated need. Your award letter will say whether you were offered it.

Comparing real costs

Using a net price calculator the right way

Sticker price isn't the real price

A college's published tuition rarely reflects what most students actually pay after grants and scholarships. A net price calculator estimates your cost at that specific school once that aid is factored in.

Find your school’s net price calculator resources →

Run it for every school on your list

Net price calculators are school-specific, so a result from one college tells you nothing about another. Run the calculator separately for each school you're considering before comparing costs.

Repeat for each school

Have your tax and income info handy

These calculators ask for household income, family size and sometimes assets, similar to what the FAFSA asks for. Having last year's tax return nearby makes the estimate more accurate.

Gather your tax return first

Treat the result as an estimate

A net price calculator gives you a planning estimate, not a guaranteed award. Your actual aid offer can differ once a school reviews your full FAFSA and any school-specific forms.

Don't forget your state

State grant aid is separate from federal aid

Your state likely has its own grant programs

Most states run their own need-based or merit-based grant programs for residents attending college in-state (and sometimes out-of-state). These are awarded separately from federal aid and often require nothing beyond the FAFSA to be considered.

Search "[your state] higher education grant agency"

State deadlines are frequently earlier

Because state grant funds can be limited, many states set an earlier priority deadline than either the federal deadline or your school's own deadline. Filing your FAFSA early helps you meet all three.

Confirm your state's deadline

Find your state's aid agency

Every state has an agency that administers its own student aid programs. Searching your state's name plus "student aid agency" or "higher education grants" will point you to the official source.

Search by state
FAFSA is free — never pay anyone to file it for you. There is no fee to submit the FAFSA on the official studentaid.gov site. Be wary of any site, email or caller that charges a fee for "FAFSA help," promises guaranteed aid, or asks for your FSA ID password — those are common scam patterns. If you want help filling it out, your high school counselor or your college's financial aid office can help for free.
Checklist

Before you file

  • ✓ Create your FSA ID at studentaid.gov before the application window opens
  • ✓ Gather your (and a parent's, if you're a dependent student) prior-prior year tax return
  • ✓ List every school you're considering, not just your top choice — it's free to add more
  • ✓ File as early as the window allows; some aid is first-come, first-served
  • ✓ Re-file every year you're enrolled — aid doesn't carry over automatically
  • ✓ Check your state's own aid deadline separately — it's often earlier than the federal one
